VT340 initial DAT9 pulse-retention experiment
=============================================

Question
--------

Is the remaining CPU2-to-CPU1 retained pulse at CPU1 PC 3925 merely a
redundant scheduler accommodation now that DAT9 is explicitly resolved and
each edge hands execution to the peer processor?

Controlled experiment
---------------------

The retention was disabled while leaving clocks, DAT9 resolution, firmware,
and all device behavior unchanged.  The first exchange then followed this
timeline:

  0.564814  CPU2 1703 has pulled P1.1/DAT9 low
  0.565317  CPU2 170B has released DAT9 high
  0.565319  CPU2 170E waits for CPU1 to pull DAT9 low
  0.570763  CPU1 enters its request-delay interval with DAT9 low
  0.573868  CPU1 releases DAT9 high
  0.573869  CPU1 3922 begins waiting for a low response
  0.574897  CPU1 times out and enters fatal path 3933

CPU2's authentic low response has therefore ended approximately 8.552 ms
before CPU1 begins sampling it.  This is not a lost edge at a scheduler slice:
the resolved level is physically high throughout CPU1's wait.  The Phase 3
checkpoint fails without retention.

Result
------

The compatibility retention is still required, but its old scheduler-only
explanation was inaccurate.  It masks an unresolved startup phase/timing
mismatch.  The driver retains only this first CPU2 pulse and now documents
the exact gap; the separate CPU1-to-CPU2 latch at 1711 was removed because it
proved redundant.

The initial mismatch does not by itself explain the late 7FF2 checkpoint
race: after this first exchange, each 1701 wait synchronizes the processors,
and the final 6.900 ms inversion is reproduced independently.  It is still a
useful clue that the current model makes CPU2 bus activity arrive materially
earlier than CPU1 firmware expects.  A hardware-correct replacement requires
an identified reset, clock, or bus-phase mechanism, not another queued pulse.

Reset-to-first-DAT9 audit
-------------------------

`phase4-reset-to-first-dat9.lua` now traces both processors from CPU1's reset
release at `390e`.  It records live P1/P3 values, CPU2 external accesses, and
CPU1 SBUF writes without changing emulated state.  The measured path is:

       0 us  CPU1 releases CPU2 reset
       3 us  CPU1 enters the four-byte LK201 command routine
      17 us  CPU1 writes SBUF byte 11
     590 us  CPU2 begins the first DC323 reset sequence
   1,629 us  CPU2 begins the initial DC323 register table
   2,164 us  CPU2 begins its internal-RAM test
   3,119 us  CPU1 writes SBUF byte 8f
   6,221 us  CPU1 writes SBUF byte 13
   6,474 us  CPU2 completes its internal-RAM test
   6,478 us  CPU2 pulls DAT9 low at 1701
   6,981 us  CPU2 releases DAT9 at 1709
   9,322 us  CPU1 writes SBUF byte 81
  12,438 us  CPU1 pulls DAT9 low for its request
  15,534 us  CPU1 begins waiting for the earlier CPU2 response

CPU2 performs 37 external accesses before the exchange.  Its fixed path
contains about 1.03 ms of explicit DC323 reset delays, 0.49 ms of register
table writes, and 4.31 ms of internal-RAM test time.  There is no status poll
or other firmware-visible wait that can supply the missing 8.55 ms.

The terminal-control-module schematic closes two proposed explanations:

- CPU1 P1.0 (`P1 RESET H`) connects directly to CPU2 RST, without an RC delay,
  synchronizer, or programmable reset device on the shown path.
- CPU2 receives the ungated 12 MHz `CPUBCLK`.  The PAL-gated clock branch is
  `CPUACLK`, which drives CPU1, not CPU2.

Intel specifies only two running machine cycles of asserted reset for the
8031.  A core-level reset-release detail of that scale cannot account for an
8.55 ms separation.  Slowing CPU2 is also contradicted by the schematic and
the controlled 8 MHz experiment, which fails CPU2's native horizontal-timing
measurement in the exact 12:8 ratio.

Instrumentation correction
---------------------------

The first version of the trace exposed a separate debugger problem: querying
the MCS-51 P1/P3 state calls the driver's port input callbacks with side
effects disabled, but `cpu2_p1_r` still advanced DAT9 ownership and
`cpu1_p3_r` could consume one-shot compatibility state.  Merely displaying
the ports therefore changed the handshake.  The callbacks now advance state
only for real CPU reads.  With live port reporting restored, all timestamps
above match the trace that omitted port reads, Phase 3 passes, and the later
Phase 4 interval remains 8.268 ms versus 13.579 ms (6.900 ms inversion).
